# ORRI
Where money actually moves in ocean conservation

Project ORRI

A roadmap to identify where money actually moves in ocean conservation, restoration, and blue-tech sectors, then turn that analysis into fundable, investable, and actionable economic blueprints that unlock capital for marine projects.

1. Problem Definition

Ocean conservation and restoration systems are constrained by a hard economic bottleneck: the people doing the science, fieldwork, and tech development cannot access funding at the scale required.

Stakeholders

Restoration scientists

Marine wildlife responders

Ocean-tech founders

Policy and management agencies

Philanthropic and impact investors

NGOs dependent on donors

Current pain

Funding is project-based, slow, unreliable, and bureaucratic

Incentives do not reward outcomes

Money moves through intermediaries who do not share the incentives of ocean practitioners

Investors cannot evaluate ocean projects because the economic mechanisms are opaque

Tech founders cannot identify paying customers or pathways to revenue

Quantifiable impact

Chronic underfunding delays deployments

High-value scientific work stalls before translation

Investors avoid the sector due to unclear returns

Communities experience long-term ecological decline

Practitioners burn out or abandon the work

Existing workarounds

Grants

CSR

Donor cycles

Government programs
All are slow, small, or inconsistent.

Why they fail
They do not align incentives, do not follow cash flows, and do not solve the underlying coordination problem.

2. First Principles Breakdown

What does “unlocking blue capital” actually require?

Fundamentals

Every ecosystem intervention has a supply chain, a customer, and a cost center

Capital flows follow incentives, not intention

Investors fund predictable returns, not ecological hope

Economic models must be simple, transparent, and based on measurable processes

Any blueprint must be executable without assuming political miracles

You cannot manufacture biotech or chemistry products yourself

You can create frameworks, models, analyses, and fundable designs

Inputs required

Sector financial data

Known transaction points

Incentive structures

Business model archetypes

Restoration economics

Risk models

Interviews with stakeholders

Real-world case studies

Constraints

No lab, no equipment, no ability to prototype physical tech

Limited access to proprietary financial datasets

No institutional authority

No capital to deploy

Conclusion
The only deliverable you can personally produce is economic architecture, not physical products or interventions.

4. End State (Minimum Valuable Outcome)

A single, clear, end-to-end economic blueprint that answers the question:

Where does money actually move in the ocean sector, who pays whom, and why?

This artifact must

Identify who pays and under what conditions

Identify what they are paying for

Map the incentives

Produce a repeatable model

Serve as a funding argument for scientists, founders, and NGOs

Be self-contained, legible, and grounded in evidence

If this document exists, it is immediately actionable and fundable.

5. Roadmap (Senku Style Stepwise)
Step 1: Establish the Base State

Define the current funding ecosystem

Collect known pain points

Map existing capital channels

Document the failures of grant-based models
If this step is not concrete, nothing downstream is real.

Step 2: Gather Inputs

Collect only what is accessible with your current means

Open data on blue economy sectors

Published research

Public NGO financials

Case studies (e.g., reef credits, kelp markets, whale carbon credits, fisheries co-ops)

Conversations with scientists, founders, policy people

Investor public statements

Sector reports

No proprietary data, no gated databases.

Step 3: Build the Primitive Tool

Primitive tool 1
Cash Flow Map of a single vertical (e.g., coral restoration, whale conservation, kelp farming).
This must show

Who pays

What triggers payment

What the intervention costs

Where the incentives break

Primitive tool 2
Incentive Breakdown of each actor.
This clarifies why funding fails.

Step 4: Validate the Primitive

Compare cash flow map with real-world examples

Ask stakeholders whether the incentives are accurate

Stress-test by removing assumptions

Identify contradictions or missing parts

If you cannot validate it, it does not move forward.

Step 5: Build the Composite Tool

Combine multiple primitives into
The ORRI Economic Blueprint
that describes

How funding mechanisms scale

How incentives align

What viable financing instruments exist

How stakeholders can act on the model

This composite must reflect actual economics, not wishful thinking.

6. Assumptions Audit

Assumption
“Investors want to fund ocean restoration.”
Evidence: weak.
Counter-evidence: capital prioritizes revenue, not ecology.
Consequence: your thesis breaks unless you design a revenue model.

Assumption
“NGOs are the correct customers.”
Evidence: mixed.
Consequence: if false, your entire target market shifts.

Assumption
“Restoration work is fundable if the economics are clear.”
Evidence: partial.
Consequence: if restoration itself is not inherently monetizable, you must shift to enabling tech or regulatory pathways.

Assumption
“Scientists will adopt economic frameworks.”
Evidence: uncertain.
Consequence: if false, distribution fails.

2. First Primitive Cash-Flow Map

This is the minimal, non-negotiable cash-flow diagram for a single ocean vertical. I chose coral restoration because it exposes your blind spots instantly.

This map is deliberately brutal, because reality is brutal.

Save this as /blueprints/primitives/cash-flow-map-v1.md.

Coral Restoration Cash-Flow Map (Primitive Version)
Stakeholders

Coral restoration scientists

NGOs running restoration programs

Dive operators / tourism companies

Coastal governments / permitting agencies

Philanthropy / foundations

Insurance companies

Real estate developers

Environmental investors

Revenue Inputs (Who Actually Pays Money Today)

Philanthropy

Source: donors, foundations

Trigger: emotional appeal, conservation narrative

Failure point: unpredictable, non-recurring, no ROI

Reality: cannot scale a sector

Government Programs

Source: federal/state environmental budgets

Trigger: grants, subsidies

Failure point: competitive, slow, bureaucratic

Reality: does not create market demand

Tourism-linked Fees

Source: hotels, operators

Trigger: “healthy reef equals profit” logic

Failure point: indirect value, weak willingness to pay

Reality: rarely linked to actual restoration

Insurance-linked Payments (rare but emerging)

Source: reef insurance contracts

Trigger: storm protection value

Failure point: complex valuation, small market

Reality: promising but tiny

Corporate ESG Budgets

Source: corporations needing sustainability optics

Trigger: image risk management

Failure point: low commitment, narrative driven

Reality: not predictable capital

Cost Centers (Where Money Goes)

Coral nurseries

Restoration labor (divers, technicians)

Monitoring and data collection

Logistics, boats, gear

Admin and compliance

Science and experimental trials

Each cost center has no inherent revenue linkage.

Incentive Misalignment (Core Economic Failure)

Those who pay are not the primary beneficiaries

Those who benefit cannot be charged

Ecological success is slow, uncertain, and not directly monetizable

The people doing the work cannot capture the value they create

This is why coral restoration struggles to become investable.

Primitive Summary

Coral restoration is currently funded like charity but priced like infrastructure.
This is economically incoherent.
Recognizing this is required before designing any financing mechanism.

3. Initial Assumptions List

Save this as /analysis/assumptions-tests.md.

These are the assumptions you keep making implicitly. I’m surfacing them so you cannot hide behind them.

Assumption List (V1)
A1. “If I map incentives, investors will fund ocean work.”

Evidence: weak.

Risk: investors still require revenue or regulatory force.

Consequence: your blueprint must include hard revenue pathways, not just maps.

A2. “Ocean science is fundable through new economic mechanisms.”

Evidence: partial.

Risk: ecological outcomes cannot always be priced.

Consequence: you may need to focus on enabling industries rather than direct restoration.

A3. “Stakeholders will collaborate once incentives are clear.”

Evidence: very weak.

Risk: organizations protect budgets, turf, and brands.

Consequence: your model must tolerate competition and misaligned incentives.

A4. “I can build this with open data alone.”

Evidence: mixed.

Risk: important financial datasets may be proprietary.

Consequence: you must design around incomplete data.

A5. “Economic clarity will change behavior.”

Evidence: historically false across sectors.

Consequence: the blueprint must show pathways that do not depend on voluntary behavioral shifts.

A6. “I can produce an output that experts will use.”

Evidence: uncertain.

Consequence: the blueprint must be practical, not theoretical.

A7. “More research equals progress.”

Evidence: false.

Reality: research without artifact creation is avoidance.

Consequence: you must produce outputs on schedule.
